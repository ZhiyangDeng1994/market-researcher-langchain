# US semiconductors — primer

## Overview
- US domestic semiconductor manufacturing capacity fell dramatically from 37% of global supply in 1990 to just 12% today, setting the strategic context for the CHIPS Act and reshoring push. — *Vyrian, 'How the CHIPS Act 2025 Impacts U.S. Semiconductor Distributors,' 2025*
- The CHIPS and Science Act, signed in August 2022, provides $52.7 billion in total funding: $39 billion for manufacturing incentives, $13.7 billion for R&D and workforce development, plus a 25% Advanced Manufacturing Investment Tax Credit valued at roughly $24 billion. — *ONSI Group, 'CHIPS Act Overview: What It Means for Federal Contractors,' 2024–2025*
- Global semiconductor sales reached $627 billion in 2024, a 19% year-over-year increase driven by AI, 5G, and automotive demand; the market is projected to expand to $697 billion in 2026. — *Vyrian, 'How the CHIPS Act 2025 Impacts U.S. Semiconductor Distributors,' 2025*
- Since the CHIPS Act's enactment, more than $630 billion in private-sector semiconductor investments have been announced across 140+ projects in 28 states, projected to create over 500,000 direct and indirect US jobs, according to the Semiconductor Industry Association as of December 2025. — *Semiconductor Industry Association, 'America's Chip Resurgence: Over $630 Billion in Semiconductor Supply Chain Investments,' December 4, 2025 (cited in Wikipedia, CHIPS and Science Act article)*
- The Department of Commerce made semiconductor incentive awards totaling approximately $30.7 billion in direct funding and $5.5 billion in loans to 19 companies across 40 projects as of July 2025, covering fab construction, expansion, and modernization. — *GAO Report GAO-26-107882, 'Semiconductors,' July 2025*
- TSMC received $6.6 billion in CHIPS Act grants plus $5 billion in loans to build three advanced fabs in Phoenix, Arizona. TSMC's first fab (Fab 21 Phase 1) is now operational and producing chips using 4nm technology, including NVIDIA Blackwell AI processors — the first time cutting-edge AI silicon was produced outside Taiwan. — *ONSI Group / Manufacturing Dive / tech-insider.org, 2025–2026*
- TSMC's Arizona subsidiary reported a net profit of $150.1 million in the first half of 2025, compared to a net loss of $143.4 million in the same period of 2024, marking a key financial milestone for US-based advanced fab operations. — *Arizona Technology Council / Phoenix Business Journal, 2025*
- TSMC announced in March 2025 an additional $100 billion US investment, bringing its total Arizona commitment to $165 billion — the largest foreign direct investment in US history — covering six advanced wafer fabs, two packaging facilities, and an R&D center. — *Manufacturing Dive, 'TSMC ramps up Arizona production as AI demand drove 2025 revenue to $122B,' January 16, 2026*
- In August 2025, the US government acquired a 9.9% non-voting equity stake in Intel for $8.9 billion, funded by $5.7 billion in unpaid CHIPS Act grants and $3.2 billion from the Secure Enclave defense program, bringing total federal support for Intel to over $11.1 billion — a landmark shift from grant-based to equity-based industrial policy. — *NBC News / LinkedIn / ainvest.com, August 2025*
- Intel received the largest single CHIPS Act direct funding award: $7.86 billion finalized in November 2024, covering facility construction and modernization across fabs in Arizona, Oregon, Ohio, and New Mexico. — *Columbia University, 'Employment Impacts of the CHIPS Act,' BPEA Working Paper, November 2025*
- The SIA and BCG project that the US will triple its domestic semiconductor manufacturing capacity from 2022 to 2032 — a 203% increase, the largest projected rate of growth of any country in the world over that period. — *SIA/BCG via Digitimes, 'US to triple semiconductor manufacturing capacity by 2032, says SIA,' May 2024; Market Data Forecast, 2024–2025*
- The US semiconductor industry faces a critical workforce gap: the SIA projects a shortage of 67,000 technicians, engineers, and computer scientists by 2030, while a McKinsey report found 53% of domestic semiconductor workers were expected to leave the industry in 2024, up from 40% in 2021. — *Harvard Business School / Sourceability, 'Why the semiconductor talent shortage threatens the industry's future,' October 2025; Tom's Hardware, 2024–2025*
- Manufacturing construction spending in the computer, electronics, and electrical sector — the primary indicator of CHIPS Act fab build-out — peaked in July 2024 and has since fallen 44%, as the initial wave of fab groundbreakings gave way to equipment installation and operational phases. — *IoT Analytics, 'US manufacturing reshoring boom: What the data says,' 2025–2026*
- The CHIPS Act imposes a 10-year ban on CHIPS Act recipients expanding semiconductor manufacturing capacity in China or other countries of concern, fundamentally reshaping global sourcing strategies for companies that accept federal funding. — *Vyrian, 'How the CHIPS Act 2025 Impacts U.S. Semiconductor Distributors,' 2025*
- The top 10 global chip companies had a combined market capitalization of $9.5 trillion as of mid-December 2025, up 46% from $6.5 trillion a year earlier and 181% from $3.4 trillion in December 2023, with the top three stocks alone accounting for 80% of that total. — *Deloitte, '2026 Global Semiconductor Industry Outlook,' December 2025*

## Ideas
# Semiconductor Reshoring Shortlist

---

## 1. AMAT – Applied Materials
**Thesis:**
- Every new US fab (TSMC Arizona ×6, Intel ×4 states, Samsung, Micron) requires AMAT deposition/etch/CMP equipment — reshoring is a multi-year equipment supercycle with AMAT as the toll-road
- Fab construction spend peaking → transitioning to tool-install phase, which is AMAT's revenue moment; backlog converts as fabs reach equipment-install milestones
- Process-control and services revenue (~25% of sales) is sticky and grows as US fabs ramp, providing downside protection against capex cycle volatility  `[UNSOURCED]`
- US-headquartered, no CHIPS Act guardrail risk (restriction applies to recipients, not suppliers); positioned to benefit from *every* funded recipient simultaneously
- AI-driven logic complexity (gate-all-around, 3D packaging) expands materials-per-wafer, structurally lifting AMAT content per tool cycle

**Key Risks:** WFE spending can pause sharply in downturns; China export controls (~30% of revenue) remain binary risk; EV/EBITDA ~49× leaves limited multiple expansion room

---

## 2. LRCX – Lam Research
**Thesis:**
- Etch and deposition leader; outsized exposure to NAND/DRAM memory — Micron's $125B Boise/Clay NY expansion is a direct LRCX catalyst as the only US-HQ'd memory chipmaker receiving CHIPS funds
- Memory cycle is recovering from 2023 trough; AI inference workloads driving HBM demand structurally raises DRAM etch intensity, LRCX's core competency
- Reshoring narrative adds *domestic* demand layer on top of secular memory recovery — two tailwinds converging simultaneously
- Services/spares ~35% of revenue provides counter-cyclical floor; installed base in new US fabs locks in recurring revenue streams for 10–15 years  `[UNSOURCED]`
- Relatively cheaper than AMAT on EV/EBITDA (~53×), though spread is narrow; share buyback program returns capital during trough periods

**Key Risks:** Memory cycle can reverse quickly; heavy China exposure (~30% revenue) subject to escalating BIS restrictions; customer concentration (Samsung, Micron, SK Hynix) creates demand lumpiness

---

## 3. KLAC – KLA Corporation
**Thesis:**
- Process control (inspection/metrology) is non-discretionary — fabs cannot ramp without yield learning, making KLAC the last tool cut and first tool added in any fab build
- Increasing chip complexity (EUV layers, 3D NAND stack heights, advanced packaging) structurally increases inspection steps per wafer, expanding KLAC's served market independent of fab count
- US fab ramp creates a decade-long installed base of KLAC tools requiring consumables, service contracts, and upgrades — highest recurring revenue quality of the WFE trio
- Highest EBITDA margins in WFE (~38%); disciplined capital return track record; relatively defensive in a sector where balance sheets vary widely  `[UNSOURCED]`
- CHIPS Act-funded fabs are *greenfield* — zero legacy installed base, meaning KLAC captures 100% of inspection tool spend rather than competing with existing equipment refresh cycles

**Key Risks:** Smallest revenue base of the WFE trio limits absolute upside; premium valuation (EV/EBITDA ~50×) bakes in execution; any fab ramp delay directly defers KLAC revenue recognition

---

## 4. MU – Micron Technology
**Thesis:**
- Only US-headquartered DRAM/NAND manufacturer; received $6.1B in CHIPS Act grants for HBM and advanced DRAM fabs in Idaho and New York — structural cost support vs. Korean/Taiwanese peers
- HBM3E is the critical memory for AI accelerators (NVIDIA Blackwell, AMD MI300); Micron is ramping HBM supply as a direct beneficiary of AI capex cycle with higher ASPs and margins than commodity DRAM
- CHIPS Act 10-year guardrail restricts Micron from expanding China capacity, but simultaneously locks Korean competitors (Samsung, SK Hynix) into similar restrictions if they accept US funding — competitive moat by policy design
- Memory is a cyclical business now entering an upcycle from a historic inventory correction; US policy support reduces downside in the next trough by subsidizing fixed-cost structures
- EV/EBITDA appears high at ~14.7× on trailing — but memory EBITDA is trough-distorted; normalized cycle earnings make current price look reasonable vs. historical mid-cycle multiples

**Key Risks:** Memory is the most volatile semiconductor end-market; HBM supply glut possible if AI capex decelerates; Samsung/SK Hynix scale advantages persist; China retaliation risk (Micron was banned from Chinese critical infrastructure in 2023)

---

## 5. INTC – Intel
**Thesis (Contrarian/Deep Value)**
- US government converted $8.9B of CHIPS support into a 9.9% equity stake — alignment of federal interest with Intel survival is now explicit; sovereign backstop reduces near-term bankruptcy/liquidity risk materially  `[UNSOURCED]`
- Intel Foundry Services (IFS) is the only US-owned leading-edge foundry at scale; if geopolitical risk around Taiwan escalates, IFS becomes strategically irreplaceable and command a rerating
- $7.86B CHIPS grant covers fab modernization across 4 states; capex burden partially socialized, reducing the cash burn timeline to breakeven for the foundry segment
- New CEO (Lip-Bu Tan, March 2025) refocusing on process execution and fabless customer acquisition; 18A node is the make-or-break catalyst — any credible external customer win (Amazon, Microsoft rumored) would be a significant re-rating event
- At EV/EBITDA ~38×, Intel trades at a discount to WFE peers on a normalized basis — though earnings are negative, the asset base (fabs, IP, x86 franchise) provides a floor the market may be underpricing

**Key Risks:** Execution risk is existential — Intel has missed process nodes repeatedly; foundry losses are substantial and may not break even before 2027–2028; AMD continues to take CPU share; government equity stake signals distress as much as support; 18A yield issues could force another reset
